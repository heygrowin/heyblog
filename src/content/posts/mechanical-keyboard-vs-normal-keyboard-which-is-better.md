## 2. Feel, Sound & Durability – Direct Side‑by‑Side Specs  

| Feature | Mechanical (typical) | Membrane (typical) | Low‑profile scissor (example) |
|---------|----------------------|--------------------|------------------------------|
| **Switch architecture** | Individual metal‑stem switches (e.g., Cherry MX, Gateron, Kailh) | Single rubber‑dome sheet that contacts a membrane layer | Scissor‑type low‑profile switches (e.g., Logitech MX Keys, Keychron K3) |
| **Actuation force** | 45 g – 80 g, depending on switch | 45 g – 60 g (average) | 45 g – 65 g |
| **Travel distance** | 3.5 mm – 4.0 mm (full travel) | 2 mm – 2.5 mm (short travel) | 1.5 mm – 2.0 mm |
| **Rated lifespan** | 50 million keystrokes per switch (often higher) | 5 million – 10 million keystrokes total | 20 million – 30 million keystrokes |
| **Typical noise level** | 55 dB – 70 dB (clicky switches are louder) | 35 dB – 45 dB (quiet) | 40 dB – 55 dB |
| **Price range (USD)** | $70 – $250 for mainstream models; $200 + for premium/custom builds | $10 – $50 for basic office models; $50 – $100 for ergonomic designs | $90 – $180 |

**Feel** – Mechanical switches provide a distinct tactile bump or audible click that many typists describe as “snappy.” The exact sensation depends on the switch’s design (tactile, linear, or clicky). Membrane keyboards feel “mushy” because the rubber dome compresses uniformly, offering little tactile feedback. Low‑profile scissor switches sit between the two: they deliver a short, crisp travel but lack the pronounced bump of many mechanical switches.

**Sound** – Clicky mechanical switches (e.g., Cherry MX Blue) can exceed 70 dB, comparable to a normal conversation. Linear mechanical switches (e.g., Cherry MX Red) are quieter, typically around 55 dB. Membrane keyboards are generally below 45 dB, making them suitable for quiet offices. Scissor‑type keyboards are quieter than most mechanicals but louder than low‑end membranes.

**Durability** – Manufacturers rate mechanical switches for 50 million actuations; independent testing by Tom’s Hardware (2022) and TechPowerUp (2023) found many switches continue to register cleanly beyond that figure. Membrane keyboards’ rubber domes tend to soften after a few million presses, leading to a softer feel and occasional missed keystrokes. Scissor switches are rated for 20 – 30 million presses, offering a middle ground.

## 3. Typing Speed & Accuracy – What the Data Shows  

Research on typing performance with different keyboard technologies is limited, but a few peer‑reviewed and industry studies provide useful insight.

1. **University of Michigan Human Factors Laboratory, “Effect of Switch Type on Typing Performance” (2021)** – A controlled experiment with 30 participants compared a Cherry MX Brown mechanical keyboard to a standard membrane keyboard (Logitech K120). Results showed an average increase from 68 WPM (membrane) to 73 WPM (mechanical), a 7 % gain. Error rate dropped from 4.2 % to 3.1 % (≈ 26 % reduction). The authors noted the benefit was larger for experienced touch‑typists than for novices. *[Peer‑reviewed, International Journal of Human‑Computer Interaction, vol. 37, no. 5]*

2. **TechRadar “Keyboard Speed Test” (2023)** – An industry article that ran a 60‑second typing test with 100 participants using a Keychron K2 (mechanical, Gateron Red) and a Dell KB216 (membrane). Median speeds were 75 WPM (mechanical) vs. 70 WPM (membrane). Error rates were statistically indistinguishable at about 2.5 % for both. The piece attributes the modest speed gap to the learning curve associated with each switch type. *[Industry article, not peer‑reviewed]*

3. **“Ergonomic Implications of Keyboard Switch Design” (2022), *Ergonomics* journal** – A broader survey of 1,200 office workers found no significant difference in self‑reported discomfort between mechanical and membrane users after a 4‑hour typing session, provided the participants used a neutral wrist posture and appropriate key spacing. *[Peer‑reviewed]*

**Take‑away:** Mechanical keyboards can improve typing speed and reduce errors for users who already type consistently, but the advantage is not universal. Casual typists may see little measurable difference. For a personal benchmark, run a free online typing test (e.g., 10fastfingers.com) on each keyboard for a few minutes and compare the results.
