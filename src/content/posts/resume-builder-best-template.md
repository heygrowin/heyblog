The best resume‑builder template is the one that balances **ATS‑friendliness** (so an automated system can read it) with **visual clarity** for a human recruiter – you will usually have to sacrifice a little on one side to gain on the other.

## Understanding the trade‑offs

### ATS‑friendliness vs visual impact  
Most large employers use applicant‑tracking software (ATS) to filter CVs before a person ever sees them. An ATS parses plain text, standard headings and simple layouts. A template that is heavily stylised – with colour blocks, icons, or multi‑column tables – may look attractive on screen but can cause the parser to drop sections or mis‑read dates.  

Conversely, a purely text‑only template will almost always get through the ATS, but it can blend in with dozens of other plain documents, reducing the chance that a recruiter will linger on it. The core decision, therefore, is **how much visual differentiation you need for your target role, and how strict the employer’s ATS is likely to be**.

## Key criteria for choosing a template

When you compare templates, evaluate them against the following checklist. You can mark each point with a simple “yes/no” or a short note on how the template meets the criterion.

- **ATS compatibility** – Uses standard headings (e.g., “Work Experience”, “Education”), avoids text boxes and graphics, and keeps the file format as .docx or PDF (PDF only if the ATS is known to accept it).  
- **Industry relevance** – Creative sectors (design, marketing) tolerate more visual flair; finance, law, and engineering usually expect a clean, professional layout.  
- **Length suitability** – One page for early‑career candidates, two pages for senior roles; the template should accommodate the appropriate amount of content without crowding.  
- **Customisability** – Ability to edit fonts, spacing, and sections without breaking the underlying structure.  
- **Readability** – Font size at least 10 pt, line spacing 1.15–1.5, and sufficient margin space.  
- **Export options** – The builder should let you download as .docx and PDF, ideally with a “print‑ready” option that preserves layout.  
- **Support and updates** – Access to a help centre or community that can advise on ATS changes or template tweaks.

## Review of common template categories

### Chronological templates  
**What they are:** A reverse‑chronological list of jobs, starting with the most recent.  
**Best for:** Roles where a clear career progression matters – e.g., finance, engineering, academia.  
**Pros:**  
- ATS parsers recognise the standard “Job Title – Company – Dates” pattern.  
- Recruiters can quickly see recent achievements.  
**Cons:**  
- Little room for visual differentiation; colour schemes are usually muted.  
- Gaps in employment become more visible.

### Functional templates  
**What they are:** Focus on skills and competencies rather than a timeline of jobs.  
**Best for:** Career changers, freelancers, or those with significant gaps.  
**Pros:**  
- Highlights transferable skills, which can be useful when applying outside your previous industry.  
- Allows a cleaner visual layout with skill bars or icons (if the builder supports them).  
**Cons:**  
- Many ATS systems expect chronological headings and may flag a purely functional CV as “non‑standard”.  
- Recruiters sometimes view functional CVs skeptically, assuming the applicant is hiding something.

### Combination (hybrid) templates  
**What they are:** Mixes a brief chronological work history with a prominent skills section.  
**Best for:** Mid‑career professionals who need to showcase both experience and specific competencies.  
**Pros:**  
- Satisfies most ATS parsers because the chronological section is present.  
- Gives you space to add visual elements to the skills block (e.g., simple bullet icons).  
**Cons:**  
- Requires careful balancing; too many columns can confuse parsers.  
- May need more tweaking to keep the file size reasonable.

### Modern design templates  
**What they are:** Templates that use colour accents, subtle graphics, and sometimes a two‑column layout.  
**Best for:** Creative industries, start‑ups, tech product roles where design sensibility is part of the job.  
**Pros:**  
- Stands out in a stack of plain CVs, which can be advantageous in roles that value aesthetics.  
- Often include pre‑written sections for portfolio links or project snapshots.  
**Cons:**  
- Many ATS systems struggle with multi‑column tables; you must test the template with a parser before sending.  
- If the recruiter prints the CV, colour may not translate well, potentially reducing readability.

## Decision framework: a simple matrix

Below is a decision matrix you can fill in with a quick “high / medium / low” rating for each template type against the criteria that matter most to you. The highest‑scoring option is usually the safest bet.

| Criterion                | Chronological | Functional | Combination | Modern design |
|--------------------------|---------------|------------|-------------|---------------|
| ATS compatibility        | High          | Low        | Medium‑High| Low‑Medium    |
| Industry relevance*     | High          | Medium     | High        | High (creative) |
| Visual differentiation   | Low           | Medium     | Medium      | High          |
| Ease of customisation    | High          | High       | Medium      | Medium‑Low    |
| Length flexibility       | Medium        | High       | Medium      | Low‑Medium    |

\*You can verify industry relevance by checking recent job adverts in your field – most listings will hint at preferred CV style (e.g., “please submit a PDF with a portfolio link” for design roles).

**How to use the matrix:**  
1. List the criteria that are non‑negotiable for the job you are targeting (e.g., “must pass ATS”).  
2. Assign a weight to each (e.g., ATS compatibility = 3, visual impact = 2).  
3. Multiply the weight by the rating (convert “High” = 3, “Medium” = 2, “Low” = 1).  
4. Add the totals for each template.  
5. The template with the highest total aligns best with your priorities.

## How to test a template yourself

1. **Export a draft** – Use the builder to download the CV as a .docx file.  
2. **Run it through a free ATS checker** – Websites such as Jobscan, ResumeWorded, or SkillSyncer let you upload a CV and see how an ATS parses it. Look for missing sections or garbled text.  
3. **Print a hard copy** – Some recruiters still print CVs. Check that colours are still legible in black‑and‑white and that the layout does not collapse.  
4. **Ask a peer** – Have someone in the same industry read the CV and note whether the most important information is obvious within five seconds.  
5. **Version control** – Keep a master .docx file and create a “plain” version (no colour, single column) for applications where you know the ATS is strict.  

If any step reveals a problem – for example, the ATS checker strips out a skill section – revert to a simpler layout or adjust the template’s HTML/CSS (many builders allow you to edit the underlying code).

## What to do about it

If you are applying for a role where the employer explicitly mentions an ATS or uses a large corporate recruitment platform, start with a **chronological or combination template that is clearly ATS‑friendly**. Keep fonts simple (Calibri, Arial, or Helvetica), use standard headings, and avoid graphics.  

If the job is in a creative field and the advert encourages a portfolio link or visual presentation, you can switch to a **modern design template**, but first run the file through an ATS checker to confirm that the essential text is still readable.  

In practice, maintain **two versions** of the same CV: a plain, ATS‑optimised file for corporate applications, and a visually enhanced file for design‑oriented roles. Use the decision matrix above to decide which version to send for each application. This approach lets you meet the technical requirements of automated screening while still showcasing your personal brand where it counts.
