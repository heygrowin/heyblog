## Why latency matters more than raw speed for gaming  

### Ping, jitter and packet loss explained  

In most online games the most important metric is **ping** – the round‑trip time for a data packet between your console/PC and the game server. A lower ping means your actions appear on screen faster. **Jitter** is the variation in ping from one packet to the next; high jitter creates the “rubber‑band” feel where characters seem to jump forward and backward. **Packet loss** (dropped packets) can cause stutter or even disconnects.  

Even a router that advertises a 3000 Mbps raw throughput cannot guarantee a 5 ms ping if its internal processing adds latency or if it cannot prioritise gaming traffic. The Asus RT‑AX86U uses a 1.8 GHz quad‑core CPU and a custom “Game Boost” QoS that automatically classifies and prioritises gaming packets, keeping ping low even when other devices are streaming video.

### How to test latency on your own network  

1. **Connect a laptop or PC directly to the router via Ethernet.**  
2. Open a command prompt and run `ping -n 20 <game‑server‑IP>` (replace with the IP of a server you know, e.g., a PUBG Mobile server).  
3. Record the average ping and the “Maximum” value – the latter shows jitter.  
4. Repeat the test while another device streams a 4K video on Wi‑Fi. If the average ping rises by more than 10‑15 ms, the router’s QoS is not handling traffic well.  

You can also use free tools like **PingPlotter** or the Android app **Network Analyzer** to visualise jitter and packet loss over a longer period.

## The hardware criteria that separate a gaming router  

### CPU and RAM  

A router’s processor does the heavy lifting of routing packets, encrypting traffic, and running QoS algorithms. For gaming you want at least a **dual‑core 1.5 GHz** CPU and **512 MB of RAM**. Anything below this will struggle to keep up when multiple devices are active. The Asus RT‑AX86U ships with a 1.8 GHz quad‑core CPU and 1 GB RAM, which is well above the minimum and gives it headroom for future firmware updates.

### Wi‑Fi 6 (802.11ax) and band steering  

Wi‑Fi 6 introduces **OFDMA** and **MU‑MIMO**, allowing the router to talk to several devices simultaneously without the “air‑time” contention that slows down older 802.11ac routers. For a gamer who also has a smartphone, a smart TV, and a smart‑home hub on the same network, this matters.  

Band steering automatically moves compatible devices from the congested 2.4 GHz band to the faster 5 GHz band. The Asus RT‑AX86U’s firmware lets you lock a gaming console to the 5 GHz band, ensuring it always gets the cleaner spectrum.

### QoS (Quality of Service) implementation  

Many routers claim to have QoS, but the implementation varies. Look for:

| Feature | Why it matters | Typical implementation |
|--------|----------------|------------------------|
| **Application‑aware QoS** | Detects game traffic by port or signature, not just by device | Asus “Game Boost”, Netgear “Dynamic QoS” |
| **Custom priority rules** | Lets you manually set a console or PC as highest priority | Available on most mid‑range routers |
| **Low‑latency mode** | Reduces internal buffering to keep packets moving | Present in Asus RT‑AX86U, not in many budget models |

When you open the router’s admin page, the QoS section should show a list of active devices with real‑time bandwidth allocation. That visual feedback is a good sign that the router can actually enforce the rules you set.
